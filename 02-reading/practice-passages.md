## Passage 1: 科技与社会 | Technology and Society

### 文章 / The Passage

```
                            THE DIGITAL DIVIDE

A.  The rapid advancement of digital technology has transformed nearly 
every aspect of modern life, from how we communicate to how we work and 
learn. However, this transformation has not been uniform across all 
segments of society. The term 'digital divide' refers to the gap between 
those who have access to modern information and communication technology 
and those who do not. While the divide was once primarily about access to 
hardware, it has evolved to encompass differences in digital literacy, 
internet quality, and the ability to use technology effectively.

B.  In developed nations, internet penetration rates now exceed 90% in 
most cases. However, significant disparities remain within these 
countries. Rural communities often face slower internet speeds and less 
reliable connections than their urban counterparts. In the United States, 
for example, the Federal Communications Commission reported in 2020 that 
approximately 21 million Americans lacked access to broadband internet, 
with the majority living in rural areas. This gap became particularly 
evident during the COVID-19 pandemic, when students in underserved 
communities struggled to participate in online learning.

C.  The situation is more stark in developing nations. According to the 
International Telecommunication Union, approximately 2.9 billion people 
worldwide had never used the internet as of 2021. Sub-Saharan Africa had 
an internet penetration rate of just 36%, compared to 87% in Europe. 
Even among those with internet access in developing countries, 
connections are often slow and expensive relative to local incomes. A 
study by the Alliance for Affordable Internet found that in some African 
countries, a basic broadband connection could cost more than 40% of 
average monthly income.

D.  Beyond mere access, a secondary digital divide has emerged in how 
people use technology. Research by the Oxford Internet Institute 
distinguishes between 'utilitarian' and 'recreational' internet use. 
Higher-income individuals tend to use the internet for education, job 
searching, and professional development, activities that can enhance 
their economic prospects. Lower-income users, by contrast, 
disproportionately use technology for entertainment and social media. 
This pattern suggests that even when access is equal, the benefits of 
technology may not be.

E.  Education is perhaps the area where the digital divide has the most 
profound consequences. During the pandemic, UNESCO estimated that 1.6 
billion students were affected by school closures. Those with reliable 
internet and personal devices could continue learning remotely, while 
millions of others were effectively locked out of education entirely. 
A World Bank study suggested that the learning losses from this period 
could reduce the lifetime earnings of an entire generation of students, 
particularly in low-income countries.

F.  Governments and organizations have implemented various strategies to 
bridge the digital divide. India's BharatNet project aims to connect 
250,000 village councils with optical fibre. Rwanda has partnered with 
private companies to provide affordable smartphones and data plans. The 
European Union's Digital Compass initiative targets universal digital 
literacy by 2030. However, experts caution that infrastructure alone is 
insufficient without parallel investments in digital skills training and 
locally relevant content development.

G.  Looking ahead, emerging technologies such as satellite internet 
constellations could potentially transform connectivity in remote areas. 
Companies like SpaceX and Amazon are developing networks of low-orbit 
satellites that promise broadband speeds comparable to fibre optic 
connections. If successful, these technologies could dramatically reduce 
the infrastructure barriers that have historically limited internet 
access in rural and underserved regions. Nevertheless, affordability 
remains a critical concern — even if the technology exists, it will only 
bridge the divide if people can actually afford to use it.
```

## Passage 2: 自然科学 | Natural Science

### 文章 / The Passage

```
                      THE SECRET LIVES OF TREES

A.  For most of human history, trees were viewed as silent, solitary 
organisms — passive features of the landscape that competed for sunlight 
and water. This perception has been fundamentally challenged by a growing 
body of research revealing that trees are far more social and 
communicative than previously imagined. Through underground networks of 
fungi and chemical signals, trees share resources, warn each other of 
danger, and even recognize their own offspring.

B.  The discovery of these underground networks began in the 1990s with 
the work of Canadian forest ecologist Suzanne Simard. While studying 
young Douglas fir trees in the forests of British Columbia, Simard 
noticed that seedlings growing in the shade were surprisingly healthy, 
despite receiving very little sunlight. Using radioactive carbon isotopes, 
she was able to trace the flow of carbon between trees and discovered 
that larger, established trees were transferring nutrients to smaller 
ones through a vast network of mycorrhizal fungi — fungal threads that 
interconnect the roots of different trees.

C.  Simard named this system the 'Wood Wide Web,' drawing a comparison 
to the internet. Through this network, trees of different species can 
exchange carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and water. The transfer is not 
random; research suggests that mother trees preferentially direct 
resources toward their own seedlings. When a mother tree is injured or 
dying, it has been observed to dump its resources into the network, 
effectively passing its legacy to the next generation. Simard's work 
demonstrated that forests are not collections of individual competitors 
but interconnected communities.

D.  Beyond sharing resources, trees also communicate threats. When 
attacked by insects, many tree species release volatile organic compounds 
(VOCs) into the air. These chemical signals serve two purposes: they 
warn neighboring trees of the attack, prompting them to increase 
production of defensive chemicals, and they attract the natural predators 
of the attacking insects. Research on acacia trees in the African savanna 
has shown that when giraffes begin feeding on them, the trees release 
ethylene gas, which triggers the production of tannins — bitter compounds 
that make the leaves unpalatable. Nearby acacia trees, even those not 
being eaten, respond by increasing their own tannin levels.

E.  The relationship between trees and fungi is mutually beneficial. 
The fungi receive sugars produced by the trees through photosynthesis, 
while the trees benefit from the fungi's vastly superior ability to 
absorb minerals and water from the soil. A single teaspoon of forest 
soil can contain several kilometres of fungal threads. Some researchers 
estimate that up to 90% of plant species depend on mycorrhizal 
relationships for their survival. The fungi also serve as conduits for 
chemical signals between trees, enabling the forest to respond 
collectively to threats such as drought, disease, and pest infestations.

F.  These findings have significant implications for forestry practices. 
Traditional logging methods that remove the largest and most connected 
trees — often the mother trees — can severely damage the forest's 
communication and resource-sharing networks. Simard's research has 
influenced forestry policies in British Columbia, where new guidelines 
recommend preserving mother trees during selective logging operations. 
However, the adoption of these practices remains inconsistent across the 
global forestry industry, and many forests continue to be managed using 
methods that disregard the complex social networks within them.

G.  Perhaps the most intriguing question raised by this research is 
whether trees possess some form of awareness or intelligence. While 
scientists are careful to avoid anthropomorphizing plant behavior, some 
researchers argue that the ability to sense the environment, communicate 
with neighbors, allocate resources strategically, and respond 
adaptively to threats constitutes a form of cognition, even if it bears 
no resemblance to animal consciousness. This remains a controversial 
position, but it is one that continues to gain attention as our 
understanding of plant behavior deepens.
```

## Passage 3: 教育心理 | Educational Psychology

### 文章 / The Passage

```
                     THE TESTING EFFECT IN EDUCATION

A.  Most students preparing for exams intuitively believe that the best 
strategy is to read and re-read their notes until the material feels 
familiar. This approach, known as passive review, creates a sense of 
fluency that students mistake for genuine understanding. However, 
decades of cognitive psychology research have demonstrated that one of 
the most effective study strategies is not reviewing material, but 
testing oneself on it — a phenomenon known as the 'testing effect' or 
'retrieval practice.'

B.  The testing effect was first systematically investigated by 
psychologist Jeffrey Karpicke and his colleague Henry Roediger III in a 
landmark 2008 study. They divided university students into four groups, 
all of whom were asked to learn a list of Swahili-English word pairs. 
One group studied the list once and was tested repeatedly; another 
studied repeatedly but was tested only once. A week later, the group 
that had practiced retrieval scored 80% on the final test, while the 
group that had only re-studied the material scored just 36%. The 
difference was striking: testing oneself was more than twice as 
effective as re-studying.

C.  The psychological mechanisms behind the testing effect are not fully 
understood, but several theories have been proposed. The 'elaborative 
retrieval hypothesis' suggests that the effort involved in retrieving 
information strengthens the memory traces associated with that 
information. Unlike passive reading, which merely recognizes existing 
memories, active retrieval requires the brain to reconstruct the 
information, creating additional pathways for future access. Another 
theory, the 'contextual variability hypothesis,' proposes that each 
retrieval attempt occurs in a slightly different mental context, creating 
multiple routes to the same memory.

D.  Practical applications of the testing effect in education have shown 
promising results across various age groups and subjects. A study of 
middle school science students in Columbia, Illinois found that regular 
quizzing improved performance on unit tests by a full grade point 
compared to classes that did not use retrieval practice. Similarly, a 
large-scale study of medical students at a Dutch university found that 
those who incorporated self-testing into their study routine 
outperformed their peers by 10-15% on final examinations. These effects 
have been replicated in fields ranging from history and geography to 
mathematics and foreign language learning.

E.  Despite its proven effectiveness, the testing effect remains 
underutilized in educational settings. There are several reasons for 
this. First, many teachers and students remain unfamiliar with the 
research. Second, testing is often perceived negatively — associated 
with anxiety, judgment, and high-stakes assessment rather than learning. 
Third, the testing effect requires more cognitive effort than passive 
review, and students may avoid it precisely because it feels more 
difficult. This is ironic, as research shows that the subjective 
difficulty of a study strategy is a poor predictor of its effectiveness. 
Strategies that feel easy, like re-reading, often produce the least 
learning, while strategies that feel hard, like self-testing, produce 
the most.

F.  Researchers have also investigated how to optimize retrieval 
practice. Spacing out testing sessions over time (known as 'spaced 
practice') produces better long-term retention than massing all testing 
into a single session. Additionally, mixing different types of problems 
or topics within a study session (known as 'interleaving') can enhance 
the testing effect, although it typically feels more difficult than 
practicing one type at a time. The use of feedback after retrieval 
attempts also improves learning, particularly when the feedback includes 
an explanation of why an answer is correct or incorrect.

G.  The implications for exam preparation are clear: students who 
replace some of their re-reading time with self-testing are likely to 
see significant improvements in their performance. This could involve 
using flashcards, answering practice questions, writing summaries from 
memory, or explaining concepts to others without referring to notes. The 
key principle is that the act of retrieving information from memory is 
itself a powerful learning event, not merely a way to assess what has 
already been learned.
```
